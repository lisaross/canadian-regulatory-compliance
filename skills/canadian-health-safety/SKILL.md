# Canadian Workplace Health & Safety Compliance

## Governing Legislation by Jurisdiction

| Jurisdiction | Legislation | Regulator |
|-------------|------------|---------|
| Federal | Canada Occupational Health and Safety Regulations (under Part II of the Canada Labour Code) | Employment and Social Development Canada (ESDC) / Labour Program |
| Ontario | Occupational Health and Safety Act (OHSA) | Ontario Ministry of Labour, Immigration, Training and Skills Development |
| BC | Workers Compensation Act + OHS Regulation (WorkSafeBC) | WorkSafeBC |
| Alberta | Occupational Health and Safety Act (AB OHS Act) + OHS Code | OHS—Alberta (Employment Standards and Workplace Safety) |

## Core Employer Obligations (All Jurisdictions)

Every Canadian jurisdiction requires employers to:
1. Take every reasonable precaution to protect workers from workplace hazards.
2. Establish and enforce safety policies and procedures.
3. Provide information, instruction, supervision, and training necessary to protect worker safety.
4. Maintain equipment, materials, and premises in safe condition.
5. Investigate workplace incidents, injuries, and near-misses.
6. Report serious incidents to the relevant authority.
7. Maintain a joint health and safety committee (JHSC) or health and safety representative, depending on workplace size.

## Joint Health and Safety Committees (JHSC) / Representatives

| Jurisdiction | Committee required | Representative required | Neither required |
|-------------|--------------------|-------------------------|------------------|
| Federal | Policy committee: 300+ employees (CLC s.134.1). Workplace committee: 20+ employees (s.135) | Fewer than 20 employees (s.136) | — |
| Ontario | 20+ workers regularly employed (OHSA s.9); 20–49: min. 2 members; 50+: min. 4 members | 6–19 workers (OHSA s.8) | 1–5 workers, unless a designated substance regulation applies |
| BC | 20+ workers regularly employed (WCA s.31) | More than 9 but fewer than 20 workers (WCA s.45) | 9 or fewer workers |
| Alberta | 20+ regularly employed workers — also triggers a mandatory health and safety program (OHS Act s.13) | 5–19 regularly employed workers (OHS Act s.14) | Fewer than 5 workers |
| Quebec | 20+ workers for 21+ days in a year: prevention program + CSS + designated RSS (LSST, in force 1 Oct 2025) | 19 or fewer: action plan + health and safety liaison officer (ALSS) | — |

**JHSC composition:** Co-chaired by management and worker representatives; at least half of members must be workers not exercising managerial functions.
**Meeting frequency:** Varies by jurisdiction — verify against the governing statute. BC joint committees must meet **at least once each month** (WCA s.37(2)). Federal policy committees meet at least quarterly (CLC s.134.1(7)); federal workplace committees at least 9 times a year. Ontario JHSCs meet at least once every 3 months.
**Certified members (Ontario):** OHSA s.9(12) requires at least one certified worker member and one certified management member. Exempt: workplaces regularly employing fewer than 20 workers, construction projects with fewer than 50 workers, and projects expected to last under 3 months (s.9(13); O. Reg. 385/96 s.4). Certification requires Part 1 (Basic) and Part 2 (Workplace-Specific Hazard) training, with **refresher training every 3 years**. BC has no equivalent certification requirement — BC requires 8 hours of joint committee member education annually (OHSR s.3.27).

This skill does not cover Quebec's LSST/CNESST regime in depth beyond the table row above; treat Quebec-specific questions as needing separate verification.

## WHMIS (Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System)

WHMIS is aligned with the **Globally Harmonized System of Classification and Labelling of Chemicals (GHS)**. The Hazardous Products Regulations (HPR) were amended on 4 January 2023 (in force **15 December 2022**) to align with the **7th revised edition** of GHS, plus the Chemicals Under Pressure class from the 8th edition. The **3-year transition period ended 14 December 2025** — the amended requirements now apply in full. Health Canada is focusing on compliance promotion until **19 July 2027** (the U.S. OSHA deadline for mixtures), moving to risk-based enforcement thereafter. Avoid the label "WHMIS 2015"; it predates the current requirements.

WHMIS is implemented through:
- **Federal:** Hazardous Products Act (HPA) and Hazardous Products Regulations (HPR) — govern suppliers
- **Provincial OH&S legislation** — govern employers in the workplace

### Hazard Classes

**Physical Hazards:**
Flammable gases (including pyrophoric gases and chemically unstable gases, now folded into Category 1A), aerosols (including new Category 3 for non-flammable aerosols), oxidizing gases, gases under pressure, **chemicals under pressure** (new class), flammable liquids, flammable solids, self-reactive substances and mixtures, pyrophoric liquids, pyrophoric solids, self-heating substances and mixtures, substances and mixtures which in contact with water emit flammable gases, oxidizing liquids, oxidizing solids, organic peroxides, corrosive to metals, combustible dusts*, simple asphyxiants*, physical hazards not otherwise classified*.

\* Canada-specific classes with no GHS equivalent. The GHS **Explosives** and **Desensitized Explosives** classes are not adopted — explosives are governed by the Explosives Act.

**Health Hazards:**
Acute toxicity, skin corrosion/irritation, serious eye damage/irritation, respiratory or skin sensitization, germ cell mutagenicity, carcinogenicity, reproductive toxicity, specific target organ toxicity (single exposure), specific target organ toxicity (repeated exposure), aspiration hazard, **biohazardous infectious materials**\*, **health hazards not otherwise classified**\*.

\* Canada-specific classes with no GHS equivalent.

**Environmental Hazards:**
The GHS environmental hazards group (hazardous to the aquatic environment; hazardous to the ozone layer) was **not adopted** into the HPR — Health Canada's position is that environmental hazards are not workplace hazards. Suppliers **may voluntarily** show these classes on labels and SDSs; WHMIS permits but does not require it. SDS sections 12–15 require the headings but a Canadian supplier may leave the content blank.

### Labels

**Supplier labels** must include:
- Product identifier
- Supplier name and address
- Hazard pictogram(s)
- Signal word ("Danger" or "Warning")
- Hazard statement(s)
- Precautionary statement(s)
- Supplemental label information as required

**Workplace labels** (required when a hazardous product is decanted into another container, **or produced by the employer at the workplace**) must include:
- A product identifier **identical to that on the SDS**
- Information for the safe handling of the hazardous product
- **A statement that a safety data sheet, if supplied or produced, is available** (not required where no SDS exists, e.g. partially exempt products)

The employer must update a workplace label as soon as practicable after significant new data becomes available.

### Safety Data Sheets (SDS)

SDS must follow the 16-section GHS format:

1. Identification
2. Hazard identification
3. Composition / information on ingredients
4. First-aid measures
5. Fire-fighting measures
6. Accidental release measures
7. Handling and storage
8. Exposure controls / personal protection
9. Physical and chemical properties
10. Stability and reactivity
11. Toxicological information
12. Ecological information
13. Disposal considerations
14. Transport information
15. Regulatory information
16. Other information (including SDS preparation/revision date)

**Employer obligations:** Obtain an SDS for every **hazardous product** received (the WHMIS 1988 term "controlled product" is repealed); make the SDS readily accessible to exposed workers and to the JHSC or representative at all times during a shift; update as soon as practicable when significant new data is received. Ontario employers preparing their own SDS must update within **90 days** of significant new data (O. Reg. 860 s.18(3)). Suppliers must provide an accurate SDS at **every sale or importation**; where significant new data became
available within the 90 days before that sale or importation, HPR s.5.12(2)–(3) lets the supplier provide the
existing SDS together with a document setting out the required changes and the date the data became available.
There is no freestanding 90-day duty to rewrite an SDS absent a sale or importation. Federally regulated, BC, SK, NL, NWT, NS, NU and YT employers must additionally **actively seek an updated SDS every 3 years**.

## Workers' Three Fundamental Rights

All Canadian jurisdictions recognize three fundamental worker rights:

1. **Right to Know** — Workers have the right to know about hazards in the workplace (WHMIS, SDS, training).
2. **Right to Participate** — Workers have the right to participate in identifying and resolving safety concerns (JHSC, health and safety representatives).
3. **Right to Refuse Unsafe Work** — Workers have the right to refuse work they have reasonable cause to believe is dangerous to themselves or others.

### Refusal to Work Process (Ontario OHSA)
1. Worker reports concern to supervisor.
2. Supervisor investigates in the presence of the worker and a JHSC member.
3. If dispute persists, Ministry of Labour inspector is called.
4. Inspector investigates and issues a decision.
5. Worker may not be penalized for exercising the right of refusal in good faith.

## Workplace Incident Reporting Requirements

### Critical Injuries / Fatalities (report immediately)
"Critical injury" is an **Ontario** term of art, not a pan-Canadian standard — BC uses "**serious injury**" (life-threatening or permanently disabling — WCA s.68, Guideline G-P2-68-1); Alberta uses **death or hospital admission** plus enumerated events, reported "as soon as possible." Ontario, BC and Alberta all require prompt notification to the regulator for the most serious events, but the trigger definitions and speed standards differ — verify against the governing statute rather than treating the list below as portable.

In **Ontario**, "critical injury" is defined in O. Reg. 420/21 (which revoked R.R.O. 1990, Reg. 834 on 1 July 2021) as an injury of a serious nature that:
(a) places the life of the injured person in jeopardy;
(b) produces unconsciousness;
(c) results in substantial loss of blood;
(d) involves the fracture of a leg or arm, but not a finger or toe;
(e) involves the amputation of a leg, arm, hand or foot, but not a finger or toe;
(f) consists of burns to a major portion of the body; or
(g) causes the loss of sight in an eye.

MLITSD reads "leg" to include the ankle/foot and "arm" to include the wrist/hand, and treats a fracture or amputation of **more than one** finger or toe as critical.

**Do not disturb the scene** (except to assist injured workers or prevent further injury) until authorized by an inspector.

### Written Reports
- **Ontario:** immediate notice by telephone or other direct means to an MLITSD inspector, the JHSC/HSR and the union for a fatality or critical injury, followed by a **written report within 48 hours** to a Director **and to the JHSC/HSR and the union, if any**
(OHSA s.51(1); contents prescribed by O. Reg. 420/21 s.3(2)). For a non-critical injury requiring medical attention or disabling a worker from usual work: **written notice within 4 days** to the JHSC/HSR and union (to the Director only if an inspector requires it) (s.52(1)). Occupational illness: written notice within 4 days to the Director, JHSC/HSR and union (s.52(2)). Retain copies **3 years**.
- **WSIB Form 7:** must be **received by the WSIB within three business days** (excluding statutory holidays) of the reporting obligation arising — triggered by health care beyond first aid, absence from work, reduced pay, modified work at less than regular pay, or modified work at regular pay for more than **seven calendar days**. Late filing: **$250**; over 30 calendar days: **$1,000**. Fatalities: call 1-800-387-0750 as soon as possible.
- **BC:** **immediate** notification to WorkSafeBC for serious injury or death, major structural failure or collapse, major release of a hazardous substance, a fire or explosion with potential to cause serious injury, or a blasting incident causing injury (WCA s.68). **Preliminary** investigation report **completed within 48 hours** — given to the joint committee/worker rep or posted; sent to WorkSafeBC **only on request** (s.71). **Full** investigation report **submitted to WorkSafeBC within 30 days** and, within those same 30 days,
**provided to the joint committee or worker health and safety representative — or posted at the workplace if
there is neither** (s.72(2)(b)–(c)); extensions available (s.72(3)).
- **Alberta:** report by phone to the OHS Contact Centre (1-866-415-8690) **as soon as possible** for a death, a hospital admission (beyond emergency room or urgent care), an unplanned explosion/fire/flood that caused or could have caused serious injury, a crane/derrick/hoist collapse or upset, or a building/structural collapse or failure (OHS Act s.33). **Potentially serious incidents (PSIs)** are reported online **after the investigation is complete** (s.33(6)) — immediate PSI reporting is no longer required. There is **no fixed deadline** for the investigation report; once complete, a copy must be **provided to a
Director** and to the HSC or health and safety representative (or, where there is neither, made available to
workers) (s.33(6)(d)), produced to an OHS officer on request, and **retained at least 2 years**.

### Near-Miss Reporting
All jurisdictions encourage near-miss reporting (incidents with potential for injury but where no injury occurred). Near-miss investigation is a leading indicator of safety performance. Establish a no-blame reporting culture.

## Due Diligence Defense

An employer facing a regulatory charge may avoid liability by demonstrating **due diligence** — that the employer took every reasonable precaution in the circumstances to prevent the incident. Evidence supporting due diligence includes:
- Written safety policies and procedures
- Regular workplace inspections
- Worker training records
- Maintenance and equipment inspection logs
- JHSC meeting minutes and action items
- Documented investigation of prior incidents and corrective actions
- Supervisor accountability mechanisms

## Criminal Liability — Bill C-45 (the Westray Bill)

Bill C-45 (S.C. 2003, c.21) amended the Criminal Code effective **31 March 2004**:
- **s.217.1** — everyone who undertakes, or has the authority, to direct how another person does work is under a **legal duty to take reasonable steps to prevent bodily harm** to that person or any other person.
- **s.22.1** — organizational liability for negligence-based offences, where a representative is a party and a senior officer (or senior officers collectively) departs **markedly** from the reasonable standard of care.
- **s.22.2** — organizational liability for fault-based offences committed by a senior officer with intent at least in part to benefit the organization.
- **s.718.21** — organizational sentencing factors; **s.732.1(3.1)** — probation conditions.

Summary-conviction fines on an organization rose from $25,000 to **$100,000**; there is **no cap** for indictable offences. Regulatory due diligence and criminal liability are separate exposures — a record that satisfies an OHSA prosecution does not automatically answer a s.217.1 charge.

## Federal: Harassment and Violence Prevention

The **Work Place Harassment and Violence Prevention Regulations (SOR/2020-130)** came into force **1 January 2021**, replacing COHSR Part XX. Employers under CLC Part II must:
- jointly carry out a **workplace assessment** with the applicable partner (risk factors + preventive measures), reviewed at least **every 3 years**;
- jointly develop a **prevention policy**, made available to all employees, reviewed at least every 3 years;
- provide **training** to all employees, the designated recipient and the employer — at least every **3 years**,
  and additionally **within 3 months of an employee's start date**, whenever the training material is updated
  or an employee is assigned to a role with different risk exposure, and, for a **designated recipient**,
  **before they assume those duties** (SOR/2020-130 s.12);
- designate a **recipient** for notices of occurrence; contact the principal party **within 7 days**; begin negotiated resolution **no later than 45 days** after notice; complete the resolution process **within 1 year**; provide **monthly status updates**;
- retain health and safety records for **10 years**;
- file an **annual report** with the Head of Compliance and Enforcement **on or before March 1** each year.

Policy committees, workplace committees and representatives are **barred** from participating in harassment and violence investigations (CLC s.134.1(4.1)).
